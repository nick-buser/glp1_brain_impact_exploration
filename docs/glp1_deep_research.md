# How GLP-1 Receptor Agonists Reshape the Brain: A Mechanism-Focused Synthesis on Reward, Affect, and Hedonic Tone

## Opening orientation

Glucagon-like peptide-1 (GLP-1) receptor agonists — semaglutide, liraglutide, exenatide, dulaglutide, and the dual GIP/GLP-1 agonist tirzepatide — were designed as incretin-mimetic peripheral metabolic drugs. They have turned out to be, almost incidentally, among the most consequential neuroactive compounds introduced in the last decade. They reduce food intake, dampen craving for several drugs of abuse, generate observational signals of reduced suicidality and possibly reduced Alzheimer's risk, *and* generate countervailing signals of emotional blunting, anhedonia, and dose-dependent anxiogenesis in animals. Coherently explaining this surface contradiction requires moving past the "they make you eat less" pamphlet level into the actual circuit pharmacology — and into honest acknowledgement of what is still genuinely undetermined.

The story that emerges, and that I will build progressively below, is roughly this: GLP-1 in the brain is best understood as a *metabolic-and-aversive interoceptive satiety signal* that down-regulates the gain of the mesolimbic incentive-salience system — i.e., the dopaminergic "wanting" channel — across reward types, while engaging stress, autonomic, and aversive circuits in the brainstem, amygdala, and PVN. That single-mechanism sketch elegantly explains decreased food and drug craving, plausibly explains some emotional flattening and certain anhedonia anecdotes, and tells us where to look for unintended long-term effects. But it does not yet explain the apparent paradoxes — for example, that systemic semaglutide can *enhance* VTA dopamine during sucrose consumption in some recent rodent recordings even as it suppresses motivation, or that observational pharmacovigilance data point toward *lower* not higher suicidal ideation. These tensions matter and are addressed below.

## 1. Receptor neuroanatomy: where the drugs act, and on whom

GLP-1 receptors (GLP-1R) are class B G protein–coupled receptors that signal predominantly through Gαs and cAMP, with some β-arrestin and Gq cross-talk. In the central nervous system their distribution is highly conserved between rodents, non-human primates, and humans, but with several quantitative and a few qualitative species differences that matter for translation.

The densest GLP-1R expression sits in regions associated with energy homeostasis and visceral interoception: the **paraventricular nucleus (PVN)** and **arcuate nucleus (ARC)** of the hypothalamus, and the **nucleus tractus solitarius (NTS), area postrema (AP), and dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus (DMX)** in the dorsal medulla. The AP, sitting outside the blood-brain barrier, is a privileged interface for peripherally administered drug. The NTS is the principal hindbrain integrator of vagal afferent information from the gut and, importantly, also contains the brain's own GLP-1-producing neurons (the preproglucagon, PPG, population — see below). The dorsal motor nucleus of the vagus drives parasympathetic outflow.

Mesolimbic and limbic expression is selective but functionally critical: GLP-1R is present in the **ventral tegmental area (VTA)**, in **nucleus accumbens (NAc) core and shell**, and in **ventral pallidum** — the latter being notable because ventral pallidum harbors one of the canonical Berridge "hedonic hotspots" for sweetness liking, so even modest GLP-1R presence there is mechanistically interesting. GLP-1R is also expressed in the **bed nucleus of the stria terminalis (BNST)**, in the **central and basolateral amygdala (CeA/BLA)**, in **hippocampus** (with some inconsistencies between studies), in **prefrontal cortex** (sparsely), in **lateral habenula** (also sparsely, but in a region central to aversive reward-prediction-error signaling and to depression-like states), and — perhaps most strikingly — at very high density in the **dorsolateral septum**, which now appears to be a major node for liraglutide's anorectic action.

At the cellular level, mouse *Glp1r-cre* fate-mapping (Cork et al., 2015) shows that GLP-1R-expressing cells include both **glutamatergic and GABAergic** populations and include some **tyrosine-hydroxylase-positive (i.e., dopaminergic)** neurons relevant to direct dopaminergic modulation in the VTA. **Cholinergic** populations expressing GLP-1R are sparser but have been documented in basal forebrain and in certain brainstem nuclei. They are largely *not* on astroglia in the canonical reporter studies, though microglial GLP-1R expression has been suggested under inflammatory conditions. In the PVN, GLP-1R is on corticotropin-releasing-hormone (CRH) neurons, providing the direct substrate for HPA-axis activation. In the dorsolateral septum, GLP-1R neurons are largely GABAergic and send long-range inhibitory projections to the lateral hypothalamic area; in the NAc shell, GLP-1R is on medium spiny neurons.

Species differences are real and inferentially important. Heppner et al.'s macaque mapping showed that **GLP-1R expression in the amygdala is substantially higher in non-human primates than in rodents**, predicting that affective and stress effects in humans may be larger than rodent models suggest. Recent single-cell and RNAscope work on the **human nodose ganglion** (the vagal sensory ganglion) shows that *Glp1r* is expressed but at lower per-neuron transcript abundance than in mouse, raising the possibility that the peripheral vagal arm of GLP-1 signaling is relatively weaker in humans — pushing the burden of CNS effects toward direct central rather than indirect vagal action.

## 2. Pharmacology and brain access: how the molecules get in

A persistent confusion in the literature concerns whether peripherally administered GLP-1RAs cross the blood-brain barrier (BBB) in any meaningful way. The current best synthesis is that they do *not* substantially penetrate the BBB by passive or transcellular mechanisms — these are 3–5 kDa peptides — but they reach brain *targets* through three more nuanced routes.

First, **circumventricular organs** (notably the area postrema and median eminence) and **tanycytes** lining the third ventricle express GLP-1R and lie outside the tight BBB, providing direct access points. The Gabery/Knudsen group at Novo Nordisk demonstrated, using fluorescently labeled semaglutide in rodents, that the drug binds neurons in the AP/NTS, ARC, PVN, lateral septum, and a few other discrete nuclei, but does not produce widespread parenchymal staining consistent with general BBB crossing. Second, **adsorptive transcytosis** appears to allow slow brain uptake correlated with absolute net peptide charge rather than lipophilicity; Banks and colleagues, comparing thirteen incretin-receptor agonists, found liraglutide, semaglutide, and tirzepatide to have *little* meaningful entry within 1 hour of intravenous injection but slow uptake over several hours by extracellular pathways. Albiglutide and dulaglutide showed faster brain uptake in those same head-to-head studies. Third, retrograde signaling via **vagal afferents** that synapse in the NTS provides a peripheral-to-central pathway that does not require the drug itself to enter the brain.

The practical translational consequence is that the deep limbic structures with GLP-1R — hippocampus, amygdala, NAc, VTA, ventral pallidum, lateral habenula — are reached only sparsely by direct drug action. Most central effects of peripheral semaglutide and liraglutide must therefore be mediated by **second-order projections** from BBB-accessible nodes (especially the AP, NTS, and ARC) onto these deeper structures, or by relatively low concentrations of drug that nevertheless suffice at G-protein-coupled receptors. This is consistent with whole-brain c-Fos mapping showing that semaglutide activates a circumscribed network of hypothalamic, hindbrain, septal, and amygdalar nuclei rather than a diffuse cortical pattern.

Drug-to-drug differences are emerging as clinically relevant. Exenatide is small enough to enter via adsorptive transcytosis at appreciable rates and shows clear central exposure. Semaglutide's high serum albumin binding and large effective hydrodynamic radius limit BBB transit. Tirzepatide adds GIP receptor agonism, and GIP receptors have an even more restricted but partially overlapping CNS distribution; the GIP component may explain part of its additional weight-loss potency through hypothalamic and brainstem nausea/aversion buffering rather than additional reward suppression per se. Newer molecules engineered explicitly for BBB penetration (such as the dual GLP-1/GIP agonist DA5-CH) outperform semaglutide on hippocampal long-term potentiation and synapse counts in 3xTg Alzheimer's mice — suggesting that the relatively modest CNS-protective effects of current marketed drugs may be a function of pharmacokinetics rather than ceiling biology.

## 3. The endogenous central GLP-1 system: PPG NTS neurons

A crucial and often-omitted point: the brain makes its own GLP-1. **Preproglucagon (PPG) neurons** in the caudal NTS and adjacent medullary intermediate reticular nucleus express the *Gcg* gene and post-translationally produce active GLP-1, which they release at axon terminals throughout the brain — to PVN, ARC, DMH, BNST, CeA, lateral septum, NAc, VTA, and spinal cord. These are the principal endogenous source of central GLP-1; viral diphtheria-toxin ablation of PPG^NTS^ neurons collapses brain GLP-1 levels.

The functional logic of these neurons, elegantly worked out by Trapp, Rinaman, Hayes, Brierley and colleagues, is illuminating. PPG^NTS^ neurons are **not** required for ordinary ad libitum feeding. Their ablation does not change normal chow intake, body weight, or glucose tolerance. They are recruited under two specific conditions: **large meals/gastric distension** (mechanical satiation signals) and **psychogenic and interoceptive stress** (e.g., restraint stress, LiCl-induced visceral malaise). Ablation produces hyperphagia after a fast or a large preload and blocks stress-induced hypophagia. Chemogenetic activation of PPG^NTS^ neurons potently suppresses eating. Rinaman has further shown that PPG^NTS^ neurons are *suppressed* during negative energy balance (fasting), which lifts inhibition on food-seeking, drug self-administration, and exploratory behavior.

This positions central GLP-1 not as a moment-to-moment appetite regulator but as a **secondary satiation/aversive interoceptive signal** — a "stop, this is too much" or "something is wrong" message. That framing is essential for interpreting the rest of this synthesis. Pharmacological GLP-1RAs are, in effect, *sustained chronic agonism of a system that nature deploys phasically and aversively*. Whether the brain treats this as "supranormal satiety" or as "chronic mild aversive interoception" is mechanistically central to predicting affective side effects.

Recent work from Brierley and Trapp (bioRxiv, 2020/2022) further showed that **peripheral GLP-1 signaling and central PPG^NTS^ signaling are largely *parallel and additive*, not serial**: peripheral GLP-1 from gut L-cells does not principally act by activating PPG^NTS^ neurons via vagal relay; the vagal input to PPG^NTS^ comes from oxytocin-receptor-expressing rather than GLP-1R-expressing afferents. This means the two arms can be independently manipulated, which complicates the simple "peripheral GLP-1 just talks to the brain via the vagus" picture.

## 4. Mesolimbic dopamine and reward prediction error: the wanting-channel hypothesis

The mesolimbic reward circuitry — VTA dopamine neurons projecting to NAc, with feedback through ventral pallidum and prefrontal cortex — is the substrate where Berridge's distinction between *wanting* (incentive salience, dopamine-dependent) and *liking* (hedonic impact, mediated by smaller opioid/cannabinoid "hedonic hotspots" in NAc shell and ventral pallidum) becomes critical. Liking and wanting are dissociable: dopamine manipulations can powerfully alter motivation without changing the hedonic facial reactions to sucrose; opioid-receptor manipulations in NAc shell or ventral pallidum hotspots do the opposite. The dopaminergic system is also the principal carrier of **reward prediction error (RPE)** signals — the moment-to-moment difference between expected and obtained reward that drives reinforcement learning. Anything that recalibrates dopaminergic gain therefore potentially affects both motivation and the underlying reinforcement-learning algorithm.

GLP-1's effect on this system is reasonably well-characterized at the *behavioral* level, more contested at the *neurochemical* level, and only beginning to be clarified at the *cell-type/cell-resolution* level.

**Behavior.** Local microinjection of exendin-4 into the VTA, NAc core, or NAc shell of rats reduces intake of palatable food selectively, without much effect on standard chow — a phenotype consistent with attenuated incentive salience rather than blunted homeostatic hunger. Systemic GLP-1RAs reduce progressive-ratio responding for sucrose (a classical measure of motivational "wanting"), reduce conditioned place preference for palatable food, and reduce cue-induced reinstatement of food seeking. Exendin-4 reduces responding to reward-predictive incentive cues in operant paradigms (Schmidt and colleagues, bioRxiv), consistent with attenuated cue-triggered "wanting" and possibly with dampened phasic dopamine responses to predictive cues.

**Neurochemistry.** Microdialysis studies show that peripheral exendin-4 and liraglutide reduce extracellular NAc dopamine elevations evoked by alcohol, nicotine, and cocaine (Egecioglu, Jerlhag and colleagues, multiple papers 2013–2016). This is the canonical empirical pillar of the cross-reward hypothesis discussed below.

**Single-cell physiology and photometry, however, complicate the simple "GLP-1 turns down dopamine" picture.** A 2024–2025 in vivo fiber-photometry study by Kooji et al. recorded VTA dopamine activity in *Pitx3-cre* mice during a Pavlovian sucrose conditioning task. Semaglutide reduced rewards earned and licks (i.e., behavioral satiation), but did *not* attenuate VTA dopamine signaling during the cue (anticipatory) phase, and surprisingly *enhanced* dopamine signaling during the reward-collection (consummatory) phase. This is the opposite of what a naïve "blunting" hypothesis would predict and suggests a more nuanced reweighting: semaglutide may not flatten dopamine signaling globally but may shift its temporal distribution — perhaps boosting prediction-error or saliency signals during actual consumption while reducing cumulative consummatory drive through other (likely brainstem-driven aversive/satiety) channels.

A parallel preprint (bioRxiv, 2025) finds that **chronic semaglutide treatment actually *enhances* the incentive motivational value of a small food reward and its associated cue in rats** — the opposite of the cross-reward suppression story. This may reflect either dose-dependent biphasic effects, an animal pulling harder for the *diminishing remaining* food reward as overall consumption falls, or task-specific recalibration. These findings are not yet reconciled and the picture should be reported with that uncertainty intact.

A defensible synthesis: GLP-1RAs probably do *not* uniformly suppress mesolimbic dopamine. They more likely **rebalance** the system — reducing tonic incentive salience attribution to high-energy palatable/drug rewards while leaving (or even enhancing) phasic responses to certain stimuli intact. The behavioral consequence resembles incentive-salience reduction for large/rich rewards; the cellular reality is more subtle than "dopamine goes down." Whether reward prediction error signaling per se is altered remains an open empirical question that current data only obliquely address.

## 5. Human fMRI: what people's brains actually do under these drugs

A systematic review of fMRI studies of GLP-1RAs and reward-cue responding (Whittenburg et al., bioRxiv 2026, 11 included studies) finds that the great majority assessed food-cue reactivity, with only one examining alcohol cues. Across studies, GLP-1RAs (most often liraglutide, some semaglutide; tirzepatide barely studied yet in this paradigm) generally reduce activation in the ventral striatum/NAc, insula, amygdala, and orbitofrontal cortex during food-cue exposure, particularly for high-calorie food images. Effects on prefrontal cognitive-control regions are inconsistent and may even go the other way (increased dorsolateral prefrontal engagement, consistent with improved top-down regulation). The single alcohol-cue study showed reduced striatal/limbic responsivity comparable to the food-cue pattern, supporting the cross-reward generalization hypothesis at the level of human brain imaging.

The evidence base is, however, small (n=11 studies), heterogeneous in design, dominated by short-duration interventions, and contaminated by the confound that weight loss itself changes reward responses. The honest summary is: human fMRI is *consistent with* a downregulation of cue-elicited striatal/limbic reactivity, but not yet powerful enough to test mechanistic predictions (e.g., consumption-phase vs anticipation-phase differences, or wanting/liking dissociations).

## 6. Cross-reward generalization: alcohol, nicotine, opioids, cocaine, gambling

The cross-reward hypothesis — that GLP-1RAs reduce maladaptive incentive salience attribution across reward classes — is now supported by converging preclinical and clinical evidence.

**Preclinical.** Exendin-4, liraglutide, and semaglutide reduce: alcohol self-administration and operant responding (Egecioglu/Jerlhag and colleagues, multiple papers); nicotine-induced locomotor sensitization, accumbal dopamine release, and conditioned place preference (Egecioglu et al., 2013); cocaine self-administration and reinstatement (Sørensen et al., 2015; multiple follow-ups); heroin self-administration and drug-induced reinstatement of heroin-seeking (Douton et al., *Addiction Biology*, 2022, with liraglutide). Brain-region-specific microinjection studies (Vallöf/Jerlhag, *Psychoneuroendocrinology* 2019) implicate VTA, NAc shell, and lateral septum in mediating alcohol-related effects. The semaglutide alcohol effect in rats is robust across sexes (Aranäs et al., *EBioMedicine* 2023) and is recapitulated in alcohol-preferring vervet monkeys (Fink-Jensen group).

**Mechanism — central GABAergic component.** Chuong, Farokhnia, Khom et al. (*JCI Insight*, 2023) showed that semaglutide's alcohol-drinking suppression involves modulation of central amygdala GABA neurotransmission, not solely dopaminergic effects. This is important: it means "cross-reward" effects are not purely a dopaminergic gain story; they recruit aversive/anxiolytic-anxiogenic circuitry as well.

**Human trials and pharmacoepidemiology — alcohol.**
- Wang, Volkow, Xu et al. (*Nature Communications* 2024) showed in a large real-world cohort of nearly 84,000 patients with type 2 diabetes that semaglutide prescription was associated with substantially lower incidence and recurrence of alcohol use disorder diagnoses compared with non-GLP-1 anti-obesity/anti-diabetic medications.
- Klausen, Fink-Jensen et al. (*JCI Insight* 2022) — exenatide once-weekly in AUD; effects modest in overall sample but pronounced in subgroup with obesity.
- Hendershot et al. (*JAMA Psychiatry*, February 2025) — phase 2 RCT of 48 non-treatment-seeking adults with AUD, low-dose semaglutide reduced laboratory alcohol self-administration, weekly alcohol craving, and (in smokers) cigarettes per day. Effects on weekly drinking days were partial — significant on some measures, not all — but the craving and lab-consumption signals were the cleanest.
- Klausen, Fink-Jensen et al. (*Lancet*, 2026) — the larger SEMALCO trial, 108 treatment-seeking patients with AUD + comorbid obesity, semaglutide for 26 weeks on top of cognitive-behavioral therapy: heavy drinking days reduced by ~13.7 percentage points more than placebo, with concordant reductions in overall consumption, craving, and liver biomarkers.

Together this constitutes the strongest randomized-trial evidence so far that a GLP-1RA reduces a non-food reward problem in humans. It is still bounded: 26-week follow-up, predominantly white populations, comorbid obesity (which may select for responders), and unclear durability after discontinuation.

**Nicotine, opioids, cocaine, gambling.** Beyond the alcohol RCTs, the human evidence is observational. Several large electronic-health-record cohort analyses (Wang/Xu group and others) suggest reduced incidence and recurrence of opioid use disorder, cannabis use disorder, and tobacco use disorder among semaglutide users — with effect sizes of ~85% odds reduction for AUD and OUD in some analyses (effect sizes that are large enough to be implausible as pure pharmacology and probably reflect substantial residual confounding). Reports of reduced gambling behavior, compulsive shopping, and nail-biting in patients on GLP-1RAs exist mostly as case reports and clinician anecdote. No randomized trial for opioids, cocaine, or gambling has yet read out. The pharmacovigilance signals are intriguing precisely because they map onto a unitary mechanism — dampened incentive salience — but the observational designs are vulnerable to confounding (people who get prescribed semaglutide differ systematically from controls, and selection effects in EHR data are notorious).

## 7. The suicidality and depression controversy

In mid-2023 the European Medicines Agency opened a safety signal review after spontaneous case reports of suicidal ideation and self-injurious thoughts from people taking liraglutide and semaglutide. The FDA in the US placed similar monitoring requirements on the labels. This generated substantial public concern.

Subsequent investigation has, on balance, *not* supported a causal link:

- **Wang, Volkow, Berger, Davis, Kaelber & Xu (*Nature Medicine*, January 2024)** analyzed TriNetX electronic health records from 240,618 patients with overweight/obesity and 1.59 million with type 2 diabetes, with propensity-score matching. In the overweight/obesity cohort, semaglutide was associated with a *lower* risk of incident suicidal ideation (HR 0.27, 95% CI 0.20–0.36) and recurrent ideation (HR 0.44, 95% CI 0.32–0.60) compared with non-GLP-1 anti-obesity drugs. The T2DM cohort replicated the direction of effect.
- The **EMA Pharmacovigilance Risk Assessment Committee (PRAC), April 2024**, concluded that available evidence does not support a causal association between GLP-1RAs (dulaglutide, exenatide, liraglutide, lixisenatide, semaglutide) and suicidal or self-injurious thoughts and actions.
- A subsequent EMA-commissioned cohort study using European real-world data (final report February 2024) reached the same conclusion: no signal of increased risk.

However, three caveats are essential. First, observational studies cannot rule out *small* effects in *susceptible subgroups*; the Wang et al. finding of *reduced* ideation could partly reflect channelling bias (patients with active mental illness may be less likely to be prescribed semaglutide). Second, the FAERS/VigiBase spontaneous-reporting signals were never going to be confirmed by cohort studies in aggregate — they were always going to require subgroup work. Third, **anecdotal reports of emotional blunting, anhedonia, irritability, and loss of pleasure in food, social, and sexual contexts** — the "Ozempic personality" phenomenon recently popularized in lay press and partially formalized in clinical case series (Singh & Singh, *Primary Care Diabetes*, 2024) — are not the same phenomenon as suicidality and may be largely missed by databases that only code formal psychiatric diagnoses. Isolated case reports of treatment-emergent mania during semaglutide dose escalation have also appeared and deserve attention because they would suggest direct neuropsychiatric effects rather than secondary consequences of weight loss.

Mechanistic plausibility cuts both ways. **In favor of antidepressant/anti-suicidality effects**: GLP-1R signaling in hippocampus is pro-neurogenic and pro-BDNF in rodent models; GLP-1RAs reduce neuroinflammation and microglial activation in multiple models; metabolic improvement and weight loss themselves can lift depressive symptoms; reduced compulsive eating and drinking can lift secondary psychiatric burden. **In favor of dysphoric/anhedonic effects**: chronic activation of an interoceptive aversive satiety signal in PPG^NTS^→PVN→amygdala→BNST pathways is mechanistically what nature uses for sickness behavior; downregulation of food and social motivation can resemble functional anhedonia even if hedonic capacity per se is intact; and rodent acute high-dose central GLP-1 is reliably anxiogenic and HPA-activating.

My read: at the population level, GLP-1RAs are probably **net neutral to mildly protective** for severe psychiatric outcomes, with a real but small subgroup of patients who experience clinically meaningful emotional flattening or anhedonia, particularly at higher doses or rapid titration. The case-report literature is consistent with this picture. Standardized prospective assessment with validated instruments (e.g., Oxford Depression Questionnaire, Snaith-Hamilton Pleasure Scale, MADRS, BDI-II) in obesity and addiction trials would resolve this in a few years; their absence in pivotal trials is a real gap.

## 8. Hedonic tone, anhedonia, and the liking/wanting/learning decomposition

Asking "do GLP-1RAs cause anhedonia?" is more usefully decomposed into three sub-questions matched to Berridge's framework.

**Wanting (incentive salience, dopamine-driven).** The strongest case for a GLP-1 effect lies here. Progressive-ratio breakpoints fall, cue-elicited responding falls, and human fMRI shows reduced cue-elicited striatal activation. Pavlovian-conditioned approach behavior, when sensitive enough to detect it, also falls. This reduction is what makes the drugs effective against compulsive consumption — and it is also what plausibly underlies subjective reports of "I just don't care about food anymore" or "alcohol no longer calls to me." Patients describe this primarily as relief; some describe it as worrying flatness.

**Liking (hedonic impact, opioid/cannabinoid-driven hotspots).** Direct evidence that GLP-1RAs reduce liking is weaker. Taste-reactivity studies (orofacial liking responses to sucrose in rats), which would be the canonical assay, are sparse for GLP-1RAs. Most behavioral evidence does not separate liking from wanting cleanly. There is no compelling rodent demonstration that GLP-1RAs degrade the orofacial liking response itself — which would argue that the core hedonic-hotspot machinery (μ-opioid receptors in NAc medial shell, anandamide in ventral pallidum) is largely intact. This is mechanistically reassuring, but it does mean that human reports of "food tastes like cardboard" are most likely reflecting reduced wanting and reduced motivational engagement rather than true hedonic-circuit damage. Whether GLP-1RAs interact with endogenous opioid or endocannabinoid tone has been only superficially examined — a real gap, given that those neuromodulators are the principal substrates of the liking system.

**Learning/reinforcement.** Reinforcement learning under GLP-1RAs has barely been studied. Given that the drugs reduce conditioned place preference, reduce cue-induced reinstatement, and may modulate hippocampal plasticity, effects on reward learning are plausible but not characterized. This is one of the largest open questions.

**Effort-based decision making.** Effort-discounting paradigms — where animals or humans choose between low-effort/low-reward and high-effort/high-reward options — would be the cleanest assay of "motivational anhedonia" of the type seen in depression and Parkinson's disease. There is currently very little published work testing GLP-1RAs in such paradigms. Sucrose preference, the most-used rodent assay, is *reduced* by GLP-1RAs but this is not pathognomonic for anhedonia (because it conflates appetite suppression and hedonic impact). This is a methodological gap of consequence: when a popular drug halves sucrose preference, we cannot currently tell whether it is doing something benign (satiety) or something more concerning (motivational anhedonia).

## 9. Stress, HPA axis, and anxiety — the dose-dependent bidirectionality

The brain GLP-1 system is *unambiguously* part of the stress circuitry. PPG^NTS^ neurons project directly to PVN CRH neurons; about half of CRF neurons in the PVN receive direct synaptic input from PPG^NTS^ axons. Central GLP-1 raises ACTH and corticosterone; central GLP-1R antagonism (with dHG-exendin or exendin(9-39)) blunts HPA responses to interoceptive and psychogenic stressors. Daily central infusion of GLP-1 *enhances* chronic-stress-induced sensitization of corticosterone responses, and conversely, daily central administration of GLP-1R antagonist blocks this sensitization — implicating endogenous central GLP-1 in the maintenance of HPA hyperactivity.

Local microinjection studies dissociate the components elegantly. Injection of GLP-1 into the PVN raises corticosterone but does not produce anxiogenic behavior on the elevated plus maze. Injection of GLP-1 into the **central amygdala** produces anxiety-like behavior without robustly engaging the HPA axis. The CeA is therefore the principal anxiogenic node, the PVN the principal HPA node, and the BNST and CRF circuitry of the extended amygdala further mediate sustained negative-affect states. CRF1-receptor activation (anxiogenic) and CRF2-receptor activation (anorectic, anxiolytic) are both engaged by central GLP-1.

This produces what initially looks like a paradox: GLP-1RAs are anxiogenic in many rodent acute paradigms, yet emerging clinical and observational data in humans show mostly neutral or slightly favorable effects on anxiety and depression. The reconciliation is dose-, route-, and timing-dependent:
- **Acute, high, central doses** of GLP-1 or exendin-4 are reliably anxiogenic and HPA-activating in rodents.
- **Chronic, peripheral, therapeutic-equivalent doses** of liraglutide and semaglutide in humans appear neutral or modestly anxiolytic in trials, perhaps because chronic agonism causes some receptor desensitization on stress circuitry, and because metabolic improvement itself is anxiolytic.
- **Sex differences** matter: Richard, Skibicka and colleagues showed that central GLP-1R activation reduces operant responding for sucrose more potently in female than in male rats, with estrogen interacting. Sex-stratified human pharmacological data are still scant.

A consequential implication: the same drug that suppresses appetite by engaging a hindbrain "aversive interoceptive" signal will, in some individuals, produce subjective feelings of mild malaise, nausea (well-documented as a side effect), reduced appetite generalization to social and other rewards, and what they may describe as low-grade dysphoria. This is not pathology — it is the system working as advertised, recruiting the affective valence of satiety/visceral malaise.

## 10. Cognition, learning, and Alzheimer's disease

GLP-1R activation has clear pro-cognitive effects in preclinical models of neurodegeneration: enhanced hippocampal long-term potentiation, increased BDNF, increased synaptic density, reduced microglial activation, reduced amyloid burden in transgenic mouse AD models. The mechanistic logic — improved central insulin signaling (Alzheimer's disease has been called "type 3 diabetes" by some authors because of robust central insulin-resistance phenotypes), reduced neuroinflammation, BDNF upregulation, improved mitochondrial function — is coherent and was the rationale for clinical translation.

The translational result, however, has been **a major negative outcome that should recalibrate enthusiasm**. Novo Nordisk's **EVOKE and EVOKE+ phase 3 trials**, presented at CTAD in December 2025 and announced via topline in November 2025, randomized 3,808 adults aged 55–85 with mild cognitive impairment or mild dementia due to amyloid-positive Alzheimer's to oral semaglutide vs placebo on top of standard of care for two years. **The trials did not demonstrate superiority of semaglutide over placebo on the primary endpoint (Clinical Dementia Rating – Sum of Boxes).** AD-related biomarkers were improved by semaglutide. The 1-year extension period has been discontinued.

This negative result is important for several reasons. It indicates that, at the BBB-limited brain exposures achievable with oral semaglutide, the AD-disease-modification hypothesis derived from observational data and rodent models does not translate. It also implicitly tests — and finds limited support for — the broader claim that chronic GLP-1 agonism produces neuroprotective effects on a human timescale through neurogenesis/BDNF/anti-inflammatory pathways. It does not, however, rule out cognitive benefits in non-AD contexts (vascular cognitive impairment, post-stroke, metabolic-associated cognitive decline), and there remain ongoing trials with more BBB-penetrant compounds. An earlier exenatide trial in Parkinson's disease showed some early-phase positive signals (Athauda et al.), but larger phase 3 results have likewise been mixed.

A separate question — whether GLP-1RAs subtly affect reward-learning, decision-making, and prefrontal control in cognitively healthy people — is essentially unstudied. There are early fMRI signals of improved prefrontal regulation of food cues, but no systematic cognitive-neuroscience characterization of effects on working memory, learning rate, exploration/exploitation balance, or effort-based decision making. Given the receptor density in prefrontal and hippocampal areas, this is a non-trivial gap. Intersection with reward learning is particularly relevant: if GLP-1RAs subtly alter prediction-error magnitudes or learning rates, the long-term consequence is not merely reduced wanting in the moment but altered formation of new reward associations — a deeper recalibration of how the brain learns about rewards.

## 11. Neuroinflammation, brain insulin signaling, and broader CNS effects

Across many rodent and cellular models, GLP-1R agonists reduce microglial activation, lower pro-inflammatory cytokine release (TNF-α, IL-1β, IL-6), preserve BBB integrity in inflammatory and ischemic challenges, and improve brain insulin sensitivity. Mechanistically, GLP-1R coupling to cAMP/PKA and downstream PI3K/Akt signaling overlaps with and synergizes with insulin receptor signaling; in states of central insulin resistance, GLP-1R activation provides an alternative route to the same downstream pro-survival, pro-plasticity targets (CREB, BDNF, mTOR-mediated synthesis). These effects are consistent and reproducible at the molecular and cellular level.

These mechanisms have driven enthusiasm for GLP-1RAs in Parkinson's disease (where the exenatide-PD trial showed early-phase positive signals), AD (now substantially deflated by EVOKE), post-stroke recovery, and chronic neuroinflammatory conditions. They are also the most plausible substrate for the mild positive mood/cognition signals seen at population level — to the extent GLP-1RAs improve the metabolic substrate, they should improve mood by indirect routes (reduced neuroinflammation, improved central insulin signaling, gut-microbiome shifts that favor anti-inflammatory taxa). This is probably part of why the population-level psychiatric signal is favorable rather than adverse, despite the more focal cellular evidence that aversive/anxiogenic circuitry is being engaged.

Gut-microbiome effects deserve mention: chronic GLP-1RA treatment shifts microbiota composition toward higher Bacteroidetes and altered short-chain fatty acid production, with downstream effects on vagal signaling, peripheral inflammation, and possibly mood. This is real but the human evidence is preliminary.

## 12. Animal-model inferential caveats — being explicit about what we can and cannot say

Because so much of the mechanistic story rests on rodent work, the conditions under which rodent findings translate to humans deserve careful framing. The major caveats:

- **Species differences in receptor distribution.** Amygdalar GLP-1R is denser in primates than in rodents; nodose ganglion *Glp1r* per-neuron expression is lower in humans than mice. This argues that affective and central (non-vagal) effects may be relatively *amplified* in humans relative to rodent predictions, while vagal/visceral effects may be relatively *attenuated*. The hippocampal expression is inconsistent across rodent studies (high in mouse, lower in rat), making translation of hippocampal-dependent effects uncertain.

- **Acute vs chronic dosing.** Many of the most striking rodent findings (e.g., acute anxiogenesis from central GLP-1, acute suppression of NAc dopamine by exendin-4) use single high doses. Human therapy is chronic, low, peripheral, and dose-titrated to minimize nausea. The system's response to chronic agonism — partial desensitization, receptor trafficking, compensatory plasticity — is poorly characterized.

- **Route of administration.** Intracerebroventricular or intraparenchymal injection of GLP-1RAs achieves brain concentrations no peripheral dose ever does. Findings from such studies establish that GLP-1R activation in region X *can* produce effect Y, but not that systemically dosed humans experience that effect at clinical doses.

- **Lean vs obese animals.** Most basic-science mechanistic work uses lean wild-type rodents; most clinical use is in obese or T2DM populations. Reward circuitry differs between these states (downregulated D2 in obesity, altered VTA tone), and effects of GLP-1RAs may differ correspondingly.

- **Sex.** Female animals show greater central anorectic and motivational responses to GLP-1RAs in several paradigms; clinical pharmacology data in humans are largely pooled. Sex-stratified analyses are an essential next step.

- **Measuring "hedonic" states in animals.** Sucrose preference, the workhorse, conflates hunger, motivation, taste perception, and hedonic processing. Effort-based and orofacial-reactivity paradigms are technically harder and underused. Until we have better assays applied to GLP-1RAs at clinically relevant exposures, claims about animal "anhedonia" should be treated as suggestive rather than definitive.

Where readers should be **more confident**: that GLP-1RAs reduce wanting/incentive salience for high-energy palatable rewards in both rodents and humans; that they reduce alcohol consumption and craving in humans; that they engage hindbrain interoceptive-aversive circuitry; that they produce relatively focal central effects via circumventricular organs and slow transcytosis rather than diffuse BBB crossing; that severe psychiatric adverse outcomes at the population level are not increased.

Where readers should be **more skeptical**: that observed effects on dopamine signaling in any one paradigm generalize across paradigms (Kooji et al. found the opposite of the canonical "blunting" pattern in their photometry); that rodent acute findings translate quantitatively to humans; that the apparent cross-reward generalization reflects a single mechanism rather than several overlapping ones; that benefits seen in observational data on AD, addiction, and mood reflect causal drug effects rather than confounding. The EVOKE failure is a sobering reminder that observational neuropsychiatric signals from electronic health records can fail to replicate in randomized trials.

## 13. The integrative picture — and what is still genuinely unknown

A reasonably coherent story emerges from all this evidence. GLP-1 is biologically an interoceptive signal of metabolic surfeit and visceral stress — a "the meal is too big" and "something is wrong in the gut" molecule. The endogenous brain GLP-1 system (PPG^NTS^ neurons) is recruited phasically by large meals and by psychogenic stress to drive transient hypophagia and stress responses. Pharmacological GLP-1RAs sustain this signal chronically and at higher tonic levels than the system ever sees naturally.

The downstream consequence, integrated across hypothalamic, brainstem, mesolimbic, septal, and amygdalar nodes, is a generalized down-weighting of incentive salience attribution to consummatory rewards — most strongly for high-energy palatable foods (the proximate evolutionary target), bleeding into alcohol, nicotine, and likely opioids and cocaine through shared mesolimbic substrate, and possibly into compulsive behavioral rewards (gambling, shopping) through still-shared incentive-salience circuitry. In parallel, engagement of NTS→PVN→amygdala/BNST stress circuitry produces dose-dependent anxiogenesis acutely; chronically and at therapeutic doses, this is mostly subclinical but can manifest as nausea, dysphoria, and emotional flattening in susceptible individuals. Reduction of neuroinflammation, improvement of metabolic substrate, BDNF effects in hippocampus, and possibly mild rebalancing of brain insulin signaling likely contribute small positive effects on mood and cognition at the population level — though insufficient to disease-modify Alzheimer's at the exposures currently achievable.

The Berridge framework is the right organizing one: these are **primarily wanting-reducing drugs**. There is no strong evidence that they damage the *capacity* for hedonic experience (the small opioid/CB1 hotspots in NAc shell and ventral pallidum that mediate liking). What patients sometimes describe as "loss of pleasure" is more likely loss of *motivated engagement* and *cue-driven anticipation* than loss of hedonic impact upon consumption — a distinction that matters clinically (a wanting-blunted patient still has the substrate for joy when it arrives; a liking-blunted patient does not).

Several genuinely underdetermined questions deserve direct acknowledgment:

- **Does chronic GLP-1RA exposure cause persistent recalibration of mesolimbic gain or of reward prediction error signaling?** Receptor desensitization, downstream homeostatic plasticity, and changes to basal dopamine tone could produce a shifted hedonic set point that outlasts the drug. There are no human longitudinal neuroimaging data adequate to answer this. Anecdotally, discontinuation typically returns appetite and food preferences to baseline within weeks (much as weight rebounds), which argues against deep recalibration, but the affective-tone and reinforcement-learning domains have not been formally tracked.

- **Does the drug affect *non-consummatory* motivation — social, sexual, achievement-related?** Case reports describe reduced libido and reduced social engagement in some patients. Whether this reflects general "wanting" suppression spreading across reward classes, secondary effects of weight loss and body-image changes, or specific endocrine effects (e.g., on hypothalamic-pituitary-gonadal axis) is unknown. There are no controlled studies of effort-based decision making in humans on GLP-1RAs.

- **Discontinuation effects on reward processing.** Beyond the well-documented rebound of appetite and weight (STEP 1 extension data and others show ~two-thirds of lost weight regained within a year of stopping semaglutide), the trajectory of reward processing, craving, and affective tone after discontinuation has not been systematically studied. For addiction indications this is particularly important — if cravings rebound on stopping, the drugs become functionally indefinite therapies. The SEMALCO 26-week endpoint does not address durability.

- **Tirzepatide and triple-agonists.** The GIP component of tirzepatide changes the receptor pharmacology in ways that may modify the side-effect profile (likely reducing nausea/aversion at equivalent weight loss), but central effects of GIP receptor activation on reward are essentially unstudied. Retatrutide (GLP-1/GIP/glucagon triple agonist) and emerging compounds will further complicate the picture. None of these have been characterized in mesolimbic or affective domains the way semaglutide has.

- **Subgroup vulnerability to dysphoric/anhedonic effects.** If 1–5% of patients experience meaningful emotional blunting, who are they? Genetic GLP-1R variants, baseline reward sensitivity, comorbid psychiatric history, dose and titration speed — none of these have been formally examined as moderators. The same is true for the opposite tail: which patients experience meaningful mood benefit, and via which mechanism (metabolic improvement, anti-inflammatory, or direct reward-system effect)?

- **Whether the population-level psychiatric "halo" (lower suicidality, lower addiction, possibly lower dementia incidence in observational data) survives causal inference scrutiny.** The EVOKE failure is the cautionary precedent. Observational signals for cross-reward suppression should be expected to attenuate (though probably not vanish) in randomized trials.

## Closing frame

GLP-1 receptor agonists are not merely metabolic drugs with incidental brain effects. They are systemically administered, chronic, partial agonists of a brain signaling system that evolved to communicate satiety and visceral aversion. They reduce the motivational pull of energy-dense rewards through identifiable mesolimbic, hypothalamic, hindbrain, septal, and amygdalar mechanisms; this generalizes to alcohol and probably to other addictive rewards; this is intentional and therapeutically valuable. They engage stress and aversive circuitry in ways that explain nausea, mild dysphoria, and probably the small-but-real fraction of patients reporting emotional flattening; this is the same mechanism viewed from the other side. At the population level they appear net-protective for psychiatric outcomes, but the EVOKE failure has shown that observational neuropsychiatric signals can mislead. The cleanest mechanistic prediction — that they reduce *wanting* without damaging *liking* — fits most current data, although recent in vivo dopamine recordings warn that the simple "blunting" picture is too simple. The most important open questions concern long-term recalibration of hedonic set point and reward prediction error signaling, effects on non-consummatory rewards (social, sexual, achievement-related), discontinuation trajectories for both metabolic and reward-related endpoints, individual subgroup vulnerability, and the largely uncharacterized central effects of GIP co-agonism and forthcoming triple agonists. These are answerable with the right experiments; they have not yet been done.

For a sophisticated reader the right disposition is one of cautious, mechanistically grounded optimism about the therapeutic potential combined with explicit acknowledgment that we are still in the early innings of understanding what chronic GLP-1 receptor agonism does to a human brain over years.
